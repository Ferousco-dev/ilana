## PART A: process ethics

### Introduction

Many students think software engineering is only about writing code, building websites,
developing mobile applications or designing databases. Being a software engineer involves much
more than technical skills. It also involves making responsible and ethical decisions.

Imagine you develop software for a hospital. Doctors use it to diagnose patients and prescribe
medications. If you are careless and it calculates the wrong drug dosage, the patient could
receive the wrong treatment, which may lead to serious consequences.

Now imagine you develop software for a bank. If you intentionally leave a hidden backdoor in the
application so you can access customers' accounts later, that is not simply a programming
mistake. It is not just unethical behaviour; it is also a criminal offence.

Software engineers build systems that affect people's lives every day. Because of this, they have
a responsibility not only to produce working software but also to produce software that is safe,
trustworthy and beneficial to society.

### Definition

Software engineering ethics refers to the moral principles and professional standards that guide
software engineers in developing and maintaining software responsibly. In other words, it means
doing the right thing while designing, developing, testing and maintaining software systems.

An ethical software engineer develops software that is:
- **Safe.** It does not put users at risk.
- **Reliable.** It works correctly and consistently.
- **Secure.** It protects users' data from unauthorised access.
- **Fair.** It treats all users equally without discrimination or bias.

If ethical standards are ignored, several serious problems can occur:
- **System failures**, where software stops working or produces incorrect results.
- **Data breaches**, where sensitive user information is exposed or stolen.
- **Loss of public trust**, causing users to lose confidence in the software or organisation.
- **Legal consequences**, including lawsuits, fines and other penalties.

A software engineer should not only know how to build software but also understand how to build
software responsibly.

### Importance of ethics in software processes

Ethics should be considered from the moment a project begins until the software is deployed and
maintained. Ethics is not separate from the software process; it is part of every stage.

Ethical practices help to:
- **Protect users and stakeholders** by ensuring the software does not cause unnecessary harm.
- **Ensure honesty and transparency** by encouraging truthful communication about capabilities
  and limitations.
- **Promote accountability** by making developers take responsibility for the quality and impact
  of the systems they build.
- **Maintain professional integrity** by encouraging engineers to follow professional standards
  and always do what is right.

Ethics ensures software is developed responsibly and in the best interest of society, rather than
focusing only on completing the project or making a profit.

### Key ethical issues

**Honesty and integrity.** Provide accurate information about system capabilities: clearly
communicate what the software can and cannot do without exaggeration. Avoid misleading
stakeholders: ensure clients, users and management receive truthful and transparent information.
Report issues truthfully: disclose bugs, risks and project challenges honestly without concealing
problems.

**Confidentiality.** Protect user information: safeguard sensitive personal and organisational
data from unauthorised access. Avoid unauthorised disclosure: never share confidential
information without proper permission. Follow data protection policies: comply with
organisational and legal requirements for handling data securely.

**Quality and safety.** Software meets required standards: develop software that complies with
established quality and industry standards. Systems are safe for users: ensure the software does
not expose users to unnecessary risks or harm. Risks are properly addressed: identify, evaluate
and mitigate potential security and operational risks during development. Neglecting quality for
speed or cost, that is sacrificing software quality to save time or reduce costs, is considered
unethical.

**Intellectual property rights.** Copyright laws: respect legal protections that prevent
unauthorised copying or distribution of software. Software licences: use and distribute software
according to the terms of its licence agreement. Ownership of code and ideas: acknowledge and
respect the rightful ownership of software code, designs and innovations.

**Professional responsibility.** Follow established processes: adhere to approved software
development methods and procedures. Adhere to standards: comply with professional, organisational
and industry standards. Take responsibility for their work: be accountable for the quality,
reliability and consequences of the software produced.

### Ethics in process adherence

As software engineers, we are expected to follow established software development processes and
standards. Some people think these processes are simply guidelines that can be ignored whenever
they are under pressure. However, that is not the case.

Following a defined software process is not just a technical requirement; it is also an ethical
responsibility.

Suppose a development team decides to skip software testing just to meet a project deadline. The
software may be delivered on time, but what happens if it contains serious bugs that affect
users? That would be unethical because the team knowingly released software that had not been
properly tested.

Another example is ignoring documentation standards. If developers fail to document their work
properly, other team members may struggle to understand, maintain or improve the software in the
future. This is also an unethical practice because it creates unnecessary problems for others.

Similarly, bypassing code or design reviews to save time is unethical. Reviews are conducted to
identify errors, improve software quality and reduce risks before the software is released.

Software development processes exist for a reason. They help ensure that software is developed
safely, reliably and professionally. Process adherence is directly linked to ethical
responsibility. A responsible software engineer follows established processes not simply because
the organisation requires them, but because it is the right thing to do for the users, the
organisation and society.

### Professional codes of ethics

As software engineers, we do not simply decide for ourselves what is right or wrong. There are
professional organisations that provide guidelines on how software engineers should behave
responsibly. These organisations, often called professional bodies, develop rules and standards
that help software engineers make ethical decisions throughout their careers.

One of the most widely recognised examples is the **ACM/IEEE Software Engineering Code of
Ethics**. It emphasises several important principles.

**Public interest.** Software engineers should always act in ways that benefit and protect the
public. They should not knowingly release unsafe software and should always consider how their
work affects users and society.

**Client and employer responsibilities.** Software engineers should be honest, trustworthy and
loyal to their clients or employers. They should not hide important problems in a software system
or misuse company resources for personal gain.

**Product quality.** Engineers have a responsibility to ensure that the software they develop
meets acceptable quality standards. This includes carrying out proper testing and avoiding the
release of software that contains known defects or serious faults.

**Professional competence.** Software engineers should possess the necessary knowledge and skills
to perform their duties effectively. They should continue learning new technologies and should
avoid accepting tasks that are beyond their level of competence.

**ACM** stands for the Association for Computing Machinery. **IEEE** stands for the Institute of
Electrical and Electronics Engineers. These are internationally recognised professional bodies
that establish standards and publish guidelines, including codes of ethics, to help software
engineers practise their profession responsibly and ethically.

## PART B: team dynamics

### Introduction

Software development is rarely a one-person job. Most software projects are developed by teams
made up of people with different skills and responsibilities. For example, a typical software
development team may include software developers, system analysts, UI/UX designers, testers,
database administrators and project managers. All of these individuals must work together to
achieve a common goal.

**Team dynamics** refer to the patterns of interaction, communication and collaboration among
team members. In simple terms, it describes how well team members work together to complete a
project.

When a team has good dynamics, members communicate effectively, share ideas freely, support one
another and work towards the same objectives.

Effective team dynamics provide several benefits:
- **Higher productivity**, because team members work efficiently and complete tasks on time.
- **Better quality software**, because collaboration helps identify and correct errors more
  effectively.
- **Reduced conflicts**, since team members communicate openly, respect one another's opinions,
  and resolve disagreements professionally.

Technical skills alone are not enough to make a software project successful. The ability to work
effectively with others is equally important.

### Characteristics of effective software teams

**Clear roles and responsibilities.** Their tasks: every team member should clearly know the
specific work assigned to them. Their responsibilities: each person should understand the duties
they are accountable for throughout the project. Their contribution to the project: everyone
should recognise how their work supports the project's overall success.

**Effective communication.** Clear understanding of requirements: effective communication ensures
everyone correctly understands the project requirements. Proper coordination of tasks: good
communication helps team members organise and synchronise their work efficiently. Quick
resolution of issues: timely communication enables problems to be identified and resolved without
unnecessary delays.

**Collaboration and cooperation.** Work together toward common goals. Share knowledge and ideas:
exchanging expertise and suggestions improves problem-solving and innovation. Support each other
to improve productivity and project success.

**Trust and respect.** Team cohesion: trust strengthens teamwork by creating a united and
cooperative working environment. Openness in communication: respect encourages honest and
transparent communication. Willingness to collaborate: mutual trust motivates team members to
work together more effectively.

**Conflict management.** Open discussion: team members should discuss disagreements honestly to
identify the root cause of conflicts. Negotiation: conflicts should be resolved by finding fair
compromises acceptable to all parties. Respectful resolution: disputes should be settled
professionally while maintaining mutual respect and positive working relationships.

### Role of team dynamics in software processes

**Requirements gathering.** Clear communication between team members and stakeholders is very
important. Everyone must have a proper understanding of what the users need. If communication is
poor, the team may gather incorrect or incomplete requirements, and this can create problems
throughout the entire project.

**Design decisions.** During the design phase, collaboration is essential. Developers, system
analysts and designers must work together to decide how the software should be structured. If
team members fail to collaborate effectively, the result may be poor or inconsistent design
decisions that affect the quality of the software.

**Testing.** Testing requires close coordination between developers and testers. Testers need
accurate information to identify defects, while developers must work quickly to fix the problems
that are discovered. Without proper coordination, some defects may remain unresolved, leading to
low-quality software.

**Deployment.** During deployment, everyone involved must work in sync. Developers, testers and
operations teams need to coordinate their activities to ensure the software is released
successfully. Poor teamwork at this stage can result in deployment failures, system downtime or
unstable software.

Poor team dynamics can lead to misunderstood requirements, project delays and low-quality
software. Strong teamwork is just as important as technical skills.

### Team roles

- **Project Manager.** Plans, coordinates and monitors the project to ensure it is completed on
  time, within budget and according to requirements.
- **Software Developer.** Designs, writes, tests and maintains the software application based on
  project specifications.
- **Tester / QA Engineer.** Verifies that the software functions correctly by identifying defects
  and ensuring quality standards are met.
- **Business Analyst.** Gathers, analyses and documents business requirements to ensure the
  software meets user and organisational needs.
- **DevOps Engineer.** Automates software deployment, manages infrastructure, and ensures
  continuous integration and continuous delivery processes run efficiently.

Every team member performs specialised responsibilities that collectively ensure successful
software development and delivery.

## PART C: industry practices

### Agile in industry

Many companies use Agile methods such as Scrum and Kanban, which involve developing software in
small iterations, receiving continuous feedback, and releasing updates frequently.

**Scrum** is an Agile software development method in which work is organised into short, fixed
time periods known as sprints, usually lasting between two and four weeks. During each sprint the
development team focuses on completing a specific set of tasks, and at the end of the sprint a
working portion of the software is delivered. Scrum involves defined roles: the Product Owner,
who determines what should be built; the Scrum Master, who ensures the process is followed
correctly; and the development team, which builds the software. The method also includes regular
activities such as sprint planning, daily meetings, sprint reviews and retrospectives, all of
which support continuous improvement and effective collaboration.

**Kanban** is an Agile method that focuses on visualising and managing work as it progresses.
Tasks are displayed on a board and move through different stages such as To Do, In Progress and
Done. Unlike Scrum, Kanban does not use fixed time periods; instead, work flows continuously. It
emphasises limiting the number of tasks being worked on at a time to avoid overload and improve
efficiency. Kanban allows teams to monitor progress easily, identify bottlenecks and make
continuous improvements to the workflow.

**Characteristics:** iterative development, continuous feedback, frequent releases.

### DevOps practices

DevOps is a modern software development approach that integrates both development and operations
activities into a unified process. In traditional environments, developers are responsible for
writing and building software, while operations teams handle deployment and system maintenance.
DevOps brings these two groups together to work collaboratively throughout the entire software
development lifecycle, improving efficiency and reducing delays.

A key feature of DevOps is **Continuous Integration**, which involves frequently integrating code
changes into a shared repository where they are automatically built and tested. This ensures
errors are detected early and the software remains stable.

Another important feature is **Continuous Deployment**, which allows software to be automatically
released to users once it has passed all necessary tests, enabling faster and more reliable
delivery.

DevOps also emphasises **automation**, where tasks such as building, testing and deployment are
performed automatically using specialised tools. These are tools that help developers do work
automatically instead of manually: version control tools (Git, GitHub, GitLab); CI/CD tools
(Jenkins, GitHub Actions, GitLab CI/CD); testing tools (Selenium, JUnit); configuration and
deployment tools (Docker, Kubernetes, Ansible). This reduces manual effort, minimises human error
and accelerates the development process.

### Plan-driven approaches

Plan-driven approaches are software development methods where everything is carefully planned in
advance before development begins. Everything is clearly written and documented before and during
development. Requirements are fully specified, design is documented, processes are recorded.
Nothing is left unclear or informal. The development process must be followed exactly as planned.
Each stage must be completed before moving to the next. Changes are not easily allowed; work is
closely monitored.

**Used in:** government projects; safety-critical systems such as medical systems and aviation
systems, because these systems require high reliability and strict control.

**Characteristics:** detailed documentation; strict process control.

### Lessons from industry practices

From industry practices we learn that no single software development process is suitable for all
projects. The choice of process depends on the type of project, flexibility is required to adapt
to changes, and continuous improvement is necessary for better results. Different software
projects are not the same, and because of this they require different approaches.

Some projects are small and simple, while others are large and complex. A small project with few
requirements can be handled using flexible methods like Agile, but a large and complex project
may require a more structured and detailed approach such as a plan-driven model.

Some systems are safety-critical, such as medical or aviation software, where errors can be
dangerous. These types of projects require strict processes, detailed documentation and careful
control. Projects like mobile applications or websites may change frequently and therefore
require flexible and adaptive methods.

Projects may also differ in time constraints, budget, team size and customer involvement. Because
of these differences, a single process cannot effectively address all project needs.

**From real-world applications, we learn that:**
- No single process fits all projects.
- Process selection depends on project type.
- Flexibility is important.
- Continuous improvement is essential.
